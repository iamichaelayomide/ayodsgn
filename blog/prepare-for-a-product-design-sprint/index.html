What founders should prepare before a product design sprint

A sprint moves quickly when the team arrives with useful context, clear decision ownership, and access to the people closest to the problem.

By Michael AyomideJuly 30, 20267 min read

0% read

A product design sprint is not a shortcut around thinking. It is a way to concentrate thinking, decisions, and prototyping into a focused period. The better the inputs, the more useful the output.

Founders do not need to arrive with a perfect specification. In fact, the sprint should help resolve uncertainty. But a team that brings real evidence and makes decision ownership clear can spend its time solving the problem instead of searching for basic context.

Define the decision the sprint must unlock

“Design the app” is too broad. A useful sprint goal describes a decision or risk. You might need to determine whether new users understand the value proposition, whether a complex workflow can be simplified, or whether a proposed MVP is coherent enough to test.

Write the sprint goal as a question. For example: “Can a first-time merchant create a store and publish one product without assistance?” A strong question gives the sprint a finish line and makes it easier to decide what should not be included.

Bring evidence, not just opinions

Collect the raw material that reveals what is happening now. Useful evidence includes:

  • Customer interviews, support messages, sales objections, and usability notes.
  • Analytics showing where people arrive, hesitate, drop off, or return.
  • Existing screens, rough sketches, pitch decks, process documents, and product demos.
  • Competitors or adjacent products, with a note explaining what is relevant about each one.

Do not clean the evidence until it becomes harmless. The awkward support thread and the messy manual workaround are often more revealing than a neat summary. They show what users are actually trying to achieve.

Expose constraints early

Constraints create the shape of a realistic solution. Share the technical environment, deadline, budget range, legal or compliance needs, content dependencies, and parts of the existing system that cannot change.

Hidden constraints are expensive. If a concept depends on data the product cannot access or a process the operations team cannot support, learning that after the design is polished creates avoidable rework.

A useful constraint is specific“We need to launch quickly” is vague. “The first release must use the existing account system and be ready for a pilot with 20 customers in six weeks” can guide design decisions.

Invite the people who know and decide

A sprint needs access to two groups: people with direct knowledge and people with decision authority. The first group may include support, sales, operations, engineering, or customers. The second includes the person who can choose a direction when evidence is incomplete.

Not everyone needs to attend every session. Identify when specialist input is required and schedule it. Also name one final decision-maker. Consensus can be valuable, but a sprint should not stall because nobody knows who owns the call.

A practical pre-sprint checklist

  1. Write one primary sprint question and two or three secondary questions.
  2. Name the primary user and describe their current situation.
  3. Gather customer evidence in one accessible place.
  4. Share the current product, workflow, or prototype.
  5. List technical, commercial, content, and timing constraints.
  6. Identify subject-matter experts and the final decision-maker.
  7. Agree on the expected output: tested concept, prototype, prioritised flow, or design direction.
  8. Protect time for review and decisions during the sprint.

This preparation does not lock the team into a solution. It creates a shared starting point. The sprint can then challenge assumptions with intention and move toward a design that reflects evidence instead of the loudest opinion in the room.
